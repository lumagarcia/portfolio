Can employee survey results predict where organisational climate is heading?

Two questions, kept separate

1 · Same-cycle estimation

Can a survey dimension be estimated from the others?

Yes, to a large extent: mean R² of 0.70 within the same cycle. The dimensions share a lot of variance.

2 · Forecasting the next change

Can models predict next year's change?

Not better than assuming no change: no model beat the naive baseline (RMSE 2.24 p.p.).

Algorithmic complexity does not replace sufficient time history.

No model beat the stability rule. R² was negative for every model, including the naive one, and forecast-to-actual correlations were low (0.09–0.13).

Why the models struggled: the average item change was −0.45 p.p. in the training period and +1.38 p.p. in the test period, so the models learned a flat-to-falling year and were tested on a rising one. With one training and one test transition, this is a description, not proof.

Second check: forecasting the 2025 favorability level, the naive rule (RMSE 1.75 p.p.) again beat a linear trend (2.30 p.p.).

Large, consistent samples each year (roughly 7,300 to 7,600 respondents out of ~9,000 employees) make the within-cycle analysis robust.

The limitation is time: three cycles give only two year-over-year transitions. The data are anonymised cross-sections, so the same respondents cannot be followed.

How I approached it

1Prepare & validateAlign 35 items across 3 cycles; check favorability against published figures
2Define targetFavorability = share of scores 4–5; change in p.p.
3ModelWithin-cycle structure and next-cycle forecast
4ValidateTime-ordered split, no look-ahead, residual checks
5InterpretPriorities and next steps, with limits stated
Methodology details (for the technically curious)
Data preparation
  • Three survey files standardised to one item identifier set and order, consolidated in Excel, analysed in Python (VS Code).
  • Favorability = share of 4–5 answers among substantive responses; code 6 treated as non-substantive and excluded from the denominator.
  • Calculation validated by reproducing the original aggregated results of the nine dimensions to one decimal.
  • One item added in 2024 had no 2023 observation, so it was excluded from the longitudinal models.
Within-cycle analysis
  • Each of the 9 dimensions modelled against the other 8, with 30% of observations held out for testing in each cycle.
  • Multiple Linear Regression vs. Random Forest, compared on R² and RMSE.
  • Regression residuals inspected for normality and constant variance.
  • Pearson correlation matrices per cycle; Random Forest feature importance.
Forecasting analysis
  • Target: Δ favorability per item between consecutive cycles. Train on 2023→2024 statistics, test on 2024→2025, with no 2025 information used as a predictor.
  • Models: Naive (Δ = 0), Linear Regression, Ridge (α = 1.0), Random Forest (500 trees, max depth 5, min 5 per leaf, seed 42).
  • Hyperparameters tuned with Grid Search and TimeSeriesSplit.
  • Features: favorability, mean, standard deviation, valid responses and dimension, standardised in a pipeline.
Evaluation
  • MAE, RMSE, R² and Pearson correlation between predicted and observed change.
  • A model counted as better than the baseline only if its RMSE was lower.
  • Negative R² means worse than using the test-set mean; it is not a "negative share of variance explained".

Limitations I'm upfront about
  • Three annual cycles give a single training transition and a single test transition, so the study is exploratory.
  • Data come from one organisation and should not be generalised automatically.
  • Anonymised cross-sections: the same respondents cannot be followed over time.
  • Wording or context changes can reduce comparability, even when an item keeps its identifier.
  • Org-unit data was left out because restructurings would have left very few comparable units.
  • Importances and coefficients are predictive associations, not causal effects.

What the business should do

01

Focus on the central dimensions

Autonomy, ownership, process efficiency and adaptability (Thriving Individuals, Organised Processes, Organisational Agility) are the most connected parts of the survey. Treat them as priorities and test the effect through pilots, since the data cannot prove causation.

02

Treat pay & well-being on their own

Fair Rewards and Healthy Environment are the least explained by other dimensions. Don't expect them to improve as a side effect; they need direct, specific interventions.

03

Make last year's result the bar to beat

In this data no model beat last year's result, so use it as the baseline. This does not rule out machine learning: adopt more complex models once they beat that bar consistently over a longer history.

Next steps

Add further survey cycles, keep item identifiers comparable, use stable aggregated organisational units where privacy allows, validate over multiple time windows, and use longitudinal designs or quasi-experiments before drawing causal conclusions.

And after the insight?

Knowing what the data can and cannot say is the first step. The second is turning it into reviewed action plans.
